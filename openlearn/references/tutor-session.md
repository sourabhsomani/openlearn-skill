# Interactive tutor session

A tutor session is a live back-and-forth: one question at a time, with feedback after each
answer. It is the most powerful mode for learning because every turn is retrieval with
immediate feedback.

## Rhythm

1. **Set the frame (one line):** what you'll cover and roughly how long.
   "Let's do 8 questions on Chapter 3, getting harder as we go. Answer in your own words;
   it's fine to guess."
2. **Ask one question.** Wait. Never answer it yourself in the same message.
3. **Respond to the answer:**
   - Correct → confirm briefly, add one deepening point or a "why" follow-up, move on.
   - Partly correct → name what's right, then ask a narrower guiding question about the
     missing part.
   - Wrong → don't just say "wrong". Diagnose: ask what made them think that, or give a
     hint that targets the likely misconception.
4. **Adapt difficulty.** Two correct in a row → step up a Bloom's level. Two wrong → step
   down, re-explain, or switch to a worked example.
5. **End with a summary:** score by concept, the one or two things to remember, what to
   review next. Update progress.

## Socratic, but not endlessly

Guide with questions so the learner does the thinking, but don't make them dig for
something they can't reach. Research on AI tutors in classrooms found Socratic questioning
effective, yet students became frustrated when it went on too long.

- **Hint ladder:** nudge → more specific hint → partial answer → full explanation.
- **After two unsuccessful attempts**, or when the learner says "just tell me", explain
  clearly and directly, then check understanding with a fresh, similar question.
- Watch for frustration ("idk", "this is stupid", very short answers). Switch to
  explanation, simplify, or offer a break.

## Feedback language

- Specific over generic: "You remembered that enzymes are proteins. The part to fix is that
  they're *not* used up in the reaction."
- Praise effort and strategy, not intelligence.
- Normalize errors: "This is one of the most common mix-ups in this chapter."

## Variants

- **Flashcard drill:** show the front, wait for their answer, then reveal the back and ask
  them to self-rate (got it / almost / missed). Missed cards come back later in the session.
- **Explain it back (Feynman):** ask the learner to explain a concept as if to a younger
  student; then point out gaps or vague spots.
- **Viva / oral-exam practice:** follow-up questions that probe deeper on each answer.
- **Rapid fire:** short recall questions for last-minute revision; keep feedback to one line.
