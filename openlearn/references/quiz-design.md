# Quiz design

A quiz exists to make the learner retrieve and apply knowledge, and to reveal exactly
which concepts are weak. Every question should be traceable to a learning objective and,
for wrong answers, to a specific misconception.

## Mix of question types

Default mix for a 10-question chapter quiz (adjust to the content and to exam format if
the learner names one):

| Type | Count | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple choice (4 options) | 4–5 | Concepts, misconceptions, application |
| Short answer / fill in the blank | 2 | Recall without cues (stronger retrieval than MCQ) |
| True/False **with justification** | 1 | Misconceptions (plain T/F is too guessable) |
| Application / numerical / scenario | 1–2 | Apply and analyze |
| Explain / compare / evaluate (open) | 1 | Higher-order thinking; give a model answer |

If the learner names an exam (CBSE board, SAT, NEET, AP, GCSE, university finals), mimic
its question styles and marking.

## Bloom's spread

Aim for roughly: 30% Remember, 30% Understand, 25% Apply, 15% Analyze/Evaluate.
Shift higher for advanced learners or revision quizzes; lower for a first pass. Tag each
question with its level and concept in the answer key, because this is what makes the
revision mode work.

Useful stems:
- Remember: *Which term describes…? / State the…*
- Understand: *Which best explains why…? / What is the main difference between…?*
- Apply: *A student does X. What will happen? / Calculate…*
- Analyze: *Which piece of evidence best supports…? / What is wrong with this reasoning?*
- Evaluate: *Which approach is most appropriate for… and why?*

## Writing multiple-choice questions

Write in this order: stem → correct answer → distractors.

**Stem**
- Tests one concept. If two concepts are combined, you can't tell which one tripped the
  learner up.
- Clear enough to answer *before* seeing the options.
- Positive wording. If a negative is unavoidable, **bold** it: "Which is **not**…".
- No textbook sentences copied verbatim, because that tests recognition of wording rather
  than understanding. Rephrase or use a new scenario.

**Distractors** (the heart of a good MCQ)
- Each must be plausible to a learner who hasn't mastered the concept.
- Build them from **real misconceptions** in your concept map: a common calculation error,
  a confused related term, a half-true statement, a reversed cause and effect.
- Same length, grammar and style as the correct answer, so no clues from form.
- Mutually exclusive.
- Avoid "all of the above", "none of the above", "A and C", absolutes like "always/never"
  that give the answer away, and joke options.
- Three good distractors beat four with a silly one. If you can only find two plausible
  distractors, use 3 options.

**Correct answer**
- Vary its position (don't let B be right half the time).
- Unambiguously the best answer according to the source.

## Answer key

Put it after all the questions (or offer to reveal it) so the learner attempts first.
For each question:

```
**Q3. Answer: C** · *Understand · Concept: osmosis*
Why C: <one or two sentences tied to the source>.
Why not A: <the misconception it represents>.  Why not B: …  Why not D: …
📖 Revisit: Lesson Part 2 / textbook section 4.3
```

For open questions give a model answer plus a short marking guide (key points that earn
credit).

## Marking a learner's answers

When the learner replies with answers:
1. Score it, but lead with what they got right.
2. For each wrong answer, name the misconception and correct it briefly. Don't paste the
   whole answer key again.
3. For short/open answers, be fair: accept equivalent wording; give partial credit and say
   what was missing.
4. Summarize by concept: "Strong: A, B. Needs work: C (2/3 wrong)."
5. Update the progress record and offer targeted revision
   (`revision-and-personalization.md`).

## Interactive widgets

If a quiz widget tool is available, pass each question with its options, the correct
option, an explanation and a hint. Keep explanations short there; the widget shows them
after each answer. Still offer the concept-level summary afterwards in chat.

## Quality check before sending

- Does every question map to an objective, and are all main objectives covered?
- Could someone answer any MCQ by test-taking tricks alone?
- Is any question answerable from another question's stem?
- Are the answers actually supported by the source?
