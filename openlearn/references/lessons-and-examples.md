# Lessons and worked examples

## Lesson structure

A lesson is not a summary of the chapter. It is an explanation built for someone who
doesn't know the material yet, broken into chunks, with the learner doing something after
each chunk.

```
## Lesson: <topic>

**By the end you'll be able to:** <2–4 objectives with Bloom's verbs>
**You should already know:** <prerequisites — offer a 2-line refresher if any is shaky>

### Big picture (3–5 sentences)
Why this topic matters and how the pieces fit. Give the overall map before details
("learn before you memorize").

### Part 1: <concept>
- Plain-language explanation first, then the textbook's formal definition/notation.
- One concrete example or analogy relevant to the learner's world (age, country, interests).
- A common misconception, named and corrected ("Many students think X. Actually Y, because…").

> **Check yourself:** 1–2 quick questions (one recall, one "why"/"what if").
> Answers in a collapsed section or at the end of the lesson.

### Part 2: <concept>
...

### Connect the ideas
How the parts relate (cause → effect, comparison table, sequence). A small table or
diagram works well here.

### Key takeaways
3–6 bullets. These later become flashcard material.
```

### Writing good explanations

- **Chunk:** 3–6 parts per lesson, each one idea. If a chapter has 15 ideas, make several
  lessons and say so.
- **Concrete before abstract:** example → principle → formal definition.
- **Explain the why:** a mechanism or reason is remembered far longer than a bare fact.
- **Dual coding:** where a diagram, timeline, flowchart or table makes structure visible, add
  one (ASCII/markdown table in chat, or a visual tool if available).
- **Level-appropriate:** for younger learners use shorter sentences and everyday contexts;
  for advanced learners include edge cases and derivations.
- **Stay faithful to the source's notation and terms.** If the textbook calls it "kinetic
  energy" with symbol K, don't switch to "KE".

### Check-your-understanding questions

Mix at least two kinds across the lesson:
- Recall: "What are the two conditions for…?"
- Elaborative: "Why does … increase when … decreases?"
- Application: "Your friend says … Is that right? Why?"
- Prediction: "What would happen if …?"

## Worked examples (procedures, numericals, problem solving)

Use when the content involves a method: maths, physics/chemistry numericals, accounting
entries, grammar transformations, coding, proofs, map reading, essay structuring.

### The faded sequence

1. **Full worked example:** every step shown, each with a short reason in italics.
   Mark sub-goals ("Step 1: identify knowns", "Step 2: choose the formula").
   Add 1–2 self-explanation prompts: *"Why do we convert to metres before substituting?"*
2. **Partly faded example:** a similar problem with the last 1–2 steps left blank for the
   learner (backward fading).
3. **More faded:** only the first step given.
4. **Independent problem:** the learner solves it fully.
5. **Variation problem:** different surface features or a twist, to test transfer.

Give solutions to 2–5 separately (end of section or on request). For stronger learners
(they ace step 2), skip ahead; for struggling learners, add another full example before
fading.

### Example format

```
### Worked example 1: <problem>
**Given:** …   **Find:** …
**Step 1 — <sub-goal>:** … *(why: …)*
**Step 2 — <sub-goal>:** … *(why: …)*
**Answer:** … (with units / final form)
💭 *Explain to yourself:* why did we …?

### Your turn (faded): <similar problem>
**Step 1:** … (done for you)
**Step 2:** ___
**Step 3:** ___
```

### Incorrect examples

Occasionally include a "Spot the mistake" example: a worked solution containing one
common error. Ask the learner to find and fix it. This targets misconceptions directly and
works well in revision.

### Non-procedural subjects

For history, literature, biology description etc., "worked examples" means **model
answers**: show a well-structured exam answer to a typical question, annotate what earns
marks (point, evidence, explanation), then give a similar question for the learner to
attempt.
